Chessvault - Why?

A wise man once said that the question is “To build or not to build?” To that, I have found my answer.

It’s to build.

Why

The answer is obvious, isn’t it?

No, it’s not.

Of course, the answer is “Why not?”

But …

Silence, Reader. I am talking.

Oh, excuse me, was I talking to myself? Anyway, where was I?

Once upon a time, Earth was timeful. Timeful. Remember that time? No, you don’t. Neither do I. None of you reading this had been born yet.

The story begins somewhere around the year 625 CE1 in what you and I alike know as India.

Over the 14-ish centuries that passed by, a game emerged and evolved into the board game that we know today as chess. It evolved so much that it essentially became timeless.

In fact, it evolved so much that we decided we didn’t want to play against humans anymore. We decided to play against computers.

And so the machine came to be. First, the legendary Deep Blue, defeating Garry Kasparov, and eventually Stockfish, an engine capable of defeating even the likes of Hikaru Nakamura, Magnus Carlsen, and Levy Rozman, to name a few.

Few?

Yes, my child, a few…

You yourself and I myself included.

But why are you telling me this?

Good question.

I am telling you this because, in the end, we found a common language.

A common language between human and human.

Between human and machine.

The game of chess…

But we don’t talk chess to machines.

Yes, yes, I know. There was binary, then there was assembly, then came C, and so on.

But those languages allowed us to command the machines, not to talk to them. Well, until the 30th of November, 2022.

Chess, I would argue, was the first language that humans and computers had in common.

But computers aside, chess does not ask “Who are you?” or “What are you?” or “How do you feel?” or any of that.

It simply asks:

“What is your next move?”

But what about blind people? What about people without arms?

Chess does not care.

It simply says:

This is the position. What is your next move?

And?

That, my dear Reader, is what I find beautiful about chess.

Not the pieces.

Not the pieces?

No, not the pieces, even though that 1950 Dubrovnik set is near and dear to my heart.

Not the board.

Not the board?

I mean… is it a checkered pattern or a chessboard?

OK… continue.

And last but not least, I do not like the game itself.

You don’t like chess?

You got me there. I do not like the game.

It’s hard. It’s boring. It has a boring objective: capture the opponent’s king. FIDE might not agree with me about that, but we don’t have to agree with everything presented to us. Do we?

Then what do you like?

I like the idea. You see, “The Idea.”

You lost me at the “The Idea” part.

You see, the idea of something so simple, yet so different.

Different?

Yes. Different.

After one full turn, 400 different games. After two, 197,281 games. After three, 119,060,324 games.

OK. I’m following.

The idea that something so simple, yet so different, can bring together such different people.

Now imagine if software could do the same.

One game. Countless possibilities.

So why should we need countless tools to enjoy it?

Why not just one?
